Chapter 1

The grandma neuron

In the late 1960s, neuroscientist Jerry Lettvin joked about a “grandmother cell”: one brain cell that fires only when you see your grandmother. In 2005, researchers recording inside patients' brains found something close. Single cells fired for one specific person, whether the patient saw a photo, a drawing or just the written name.

Things you notice. Toggle them and watch the grandma neuron decide.

Real neurons, cartoon neurons

A real neuron collects signals from thousands of others and fires when they push it past a threshold. Chains of them build up meaning: edges become shapes, shapes become a face, a face becomes her.

An artificial neuron is a cartoon of that. Multiply each input by a weight, add them up, then pass the total through an activation function that decides how strongly to fire. Negative weights work like “that's not her” signals.

Why the activation step matters

Stack weighted sums without it and the whole stack collapses into one weighted sum, a single straight-line rule. The bend lets layers build curves, edges, faces and ideas on top of each other.

Concept cells are not the only place a memory lives. Each responds to a few related things, and many cells share each memory. AI is similar: most ideas are spread across many artificial neurons. Still, researchers have found units that act like concept cells, such as a feature inside one of Anthropic's Claude models that fired for the Golden Gate Bridge in text and in pictures.

Chapter 2

Two inputs, two recipes, two outputs

Each row of a matrix is one neuron's weighted sum from Chapter 1. A matrix runs a whole layer of them at once. Press play.

The arithmetic, written out

Each row of the grid is a recipe. Multiply each input by the weight in its column, then add.

2·5 + 3·2 = 10 + 6 = 16
−1·5 + 4·2 = −5 + 8 = 3

In general, rows [a b] and [c d] turn inputs x, y into ax + by and cx + dy. That is a system of linear equations, the simplest kind of polynomial.

How this connects to AI

A matrix in a language model is a huge sheet of “how much should this input influence that output?” settings. Multiplying applies every recipe at once.

Each word is first turned into a long list of numbers. Grids like this one remix those numbers again and again, with a few other steps in between, until the system produces odds for every possible next word.

Chapter 3

How the numbers get learned

Every weight starts as a random number. Training shows the model an example, measures how wrong its answer was, and nudges each weight a little in the direction that makes it less wrong. Then it does that again, billions of times.

Recipe 1 again, but the first weight is unknown: ? × 5 + 3 × 2 should equal 16.

Step size:

From one weight to billions

  • The error becomes “surprise.” In a language model, the output is the odds for the next word. The error measures how unlikely the model thought the word that actually came next was. This is called cross-entropy loss.
  • Backpropagation finds every slope at once. It traces the error backward through the layers and works out, for each weight, which way is downhill and how steep it is. That is the only job the calculus does.
  • Each step is tiny. One step moves billions of weights by a small amount. Pre-training repeats it over trillions of words.

Why step size matters

Small steps get there, slowly. Steps that are too big overshoot the bottom of the valley, land higher on the other side, and can keep getting worse. Real training usually starts with larger steps and shrinks them over time.

With one weight the valley is a simple curve. With billions, it is a landscape with billions of directions, and training only ever looks at the slope where it is standing.

Chapter 4

Words become points on a map

Before any recipe runs, every word is swapped for a list of numbers. Training nudges those numbers until words used in similar ways land near each other. Directions start to carry meaning too.

A hand-drawn illustration. Real models use hundreds or thousands of numbers per word (BERT-base uses 768), and the arithmetic only lands close to the target, not exactly on it. Two numbers are shown here as left–right and up–down.

Chapter 5

Everything becomes numbers

Words are not special. Sound, pictures, video and sensor readings can all be cut into pieces, each piece turned into a list of numbers, and fed through the same machinery.

Illustrative. In models trained on many languages, or on pictures paired with captions, words and images with the same meaning tend to land near each other. It is mostly true, not perfectly: languages with little training text get blurrier positions, and some words carry shades of meaning no translation matches.

Chapter 6

Words look at each other

A word's point on the map is the same in every sentence, but “it” means something different each time. Attention fixes that by letting each word borrow meaning from the words around it, using the recipe idea from Chapter 2.

How much “it” looks at each word

The new meaning of “it” is a weighted recipe

1. Ask and labelEach word makes a “what am I looking for?” list and a “what do I offer?” list, using learned recipes.
2. Score the matchesMultiply and add the two lists, just like Chapter 2. A good match gives a high score.
3. Turn scores into sharesScores become percentages that add up to 100%. Those percentages are the recipe weights.

Illustrative weights. Unlike Chapter 2, these weights are not fixed. They are worked out fresh for every sentence. Real models run dozens of these in parallel and stack dozens of layers.

Chapter 7

Inside one block

Chapter 6 showed what attention does. This chapter opens the box: the three lists every word makes, why a model runs many attentions side by side, how word order gets in, and what one complete layer looks like.

Queries, keys and values

Each word makes three lists of numbers with learned recipes. Its query says what it is looking for. Its key says what it offers. Its value is what it hands over if chosen. Turn the query and watch “it” pick a partner.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q × KT ÷ √d ) × V

In words: multiply each query by every key and add (a match score), divide by the square root of the list length so scores stay tame, turn scores into shares, then blend the values by those shares. Every step is Chapter 2 arithmetic. Two numbers per list here; the original Transformer used 64 per head.

Many heads, each looking for something different

One attention can only weigh things one way. So a layer runs several at once, called heads, each with its own recipes. The 2017 Transformer used 8 heads per layer. Here is where three imaginary heads send “it”.

Illustrative. Researchers have found real heads that track the previous word, the subject of a sentence, or matching brackets in code, but most heads do mixtures that are harder to name.

Word order

Attention on its own sees a bag of words: “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” look identical. The fix is to add a position pattern to each word's numbers. The original recipe uses waves of different speeds, so every position gets its own fingerprint.

Each row is one number in the list, waving at its own speed. Read down a column and you get that position's fingerprint. Many newer models use a variation that rotates the query and key instead, but the idea is the same.

One complete layer, and what it costs

  • Attention lets words borrow from each other (Chapter 6).
  • Feed-forward is a wide layer of the neurons from Chapter 1, applied to each word on its own. In the 2017 model it widened each list from 512 to 2,048 numbers and back.
  • Add and normalize keeps a copy of what came in and adds the change on top, then rescales the numbers. That lets dozens of layers stack without the signal fading.

    Chapter 10

    A team of specialists

    Making a model bigger usually makes every word more expensive to process. A mixture of experts dodges that. It splits part of each layer into many smaller “experts,” and a router sends each word to just a couple of them.

    Illustrative routing. Real routers make this choice again in every layer, and the experts are learned, not assigned topics.

    What it buys

    2017
    Google's sparsely gated mixture of experts shows that thousands of experts can work inside one network.
    2021
    Switch Transformer sends each word to a single expert.
    2023
    Mixtral 8x7B: about 47 billion weights in total, about 13 billion used per word.
    2024
    DeepSeek-V3: 671 billion in total, 37 billion used per word.

    The model can store the knowledge of a very large network while doing roughly the work of a much smaller one for each word.

    What researchers found

    • Experts are not tidy subject specialists. The Mixtral authors found little sign of a “biology expert” or a “math expert.” Routing tracked things like word type and sentence structure more than topic.
    • The router has to be kept fair. Left alone, it overuses a few favorites, so training adds a penalty that spreads the load.
    • Memory still counts. Every expert must be loaded and ready even if only two do work, so these models are cheap to run per word but still large to host.
    Chapter 11

    Is smaller smarter?

    Classic statistics teaches a sensible rule: give a model too many adjustable numbers and it memorizes its examples, like a student who memorizes the answer key and fails the real test. So a smaller model should generalize better. Large networks broke that rule, but only partly.

    Curve shapes are illustrative; they follow the patterns reported in the papers listed below, not one specific model.

    Where the rule breaks

    • Past the danger zone, bigger helped again. A huge network can fit the training text in countless ways, and training tends to settle on smooth, simple ones that also work on new text.
    • Error falls predictably with scale. Scaling-law studies found that growing the model, the data and the computing budget together lowers error along a steady curve.
    • Some abilities only show up at size. Writing working code or following long instructions improved sharply as models grew.

    Where it still holds

    • Data is the ceiling. DeepMind's Chinchilla (70 billion weights, more text) beat their own Gopher (280 billion) on the same computing budget. Many big models had been undertrained.
    • Little data still means memorizing. Tune a large model on a few hundred examples and it can parrot them back.
    • Narrow jobs favor small models. A small model trained for one task can match a giant generalist there, and runs faster and cheaper.
    • Small models often learn from big ones. “Distillation” trains a small model on a big model's answers, so the big model is still doing work behind the scenes.
    Chapter 12

    Averages, not truth

    Everything a model knows comes from the text it was trained on. Its map of meaning settles where its sources, on average, put things. So it learns what those sources tend to say, which is the norm of what they believe, not truth itself.

    The bride wore a  ?  dress.

    Source A: places where white wedding dresses are the custom. Source B: places where red is traditional, as in many Chinese and Indian weddings. Neither answer is wrong. The model reports whichever custom dominates its data.

    Bias can be reduced, not removed

    Every collection of text was written by particular people, in particular places and times. Curating more varied data, testing how the model treats different groups, and tuning it afterward all narrow the gap. None of them produces a view from nowhere, because there is no neutral dataset to train on.

    When the norm and the truth agree

    For well-documented facts, nearly all sources agree, so the norm and the truth line up and the model is usually right. Trouble starts where sources disagree, where a group is barely represented, or where the true answer was rarely written down.

    Inside the randomness dial

    The cat sat on the  ? 

    The last layer gives every possible word a raw score called a logit. Temperature divides those scores, and a step called softmax turns them into shares that add up to 100%.

    share of word i = ezi ÷ T ÷ (sum over every word of ez ÷ T)

    Why temperature 0 can still give different answers

    At T = 0 the model always takes the top word, so the same question should give the same reply. On real servers it often doesn't. Computers round after every addition, so the order of additions changes the result. Your browser just computed this:


    but

    A GPU adds up thousands of numbers in chunks. How it chunks them often depends on how many other people's requests are processed alongside yours. A different batch gives slightly different scores. When two words are nearly tied, that tiny difference can flip the winner, and the rest of the reply goes a different way. Mixture-of-experts models add another source: when an expert is full, some systems reroute or drop words depending on who else is in the batch.

    Batch-invariant math can make replies repeatable, at some cost in speed (Thinking Machines Lab, 2025).

    Why it never reaches zero

    • The model writes likely text, not checked text. Nothing inside it looks facts up.
    • Picking words involves chance. With randomness on, the same question can get different answers.
    • Turning randomness off doesn't fix it. When the odds are flat, the single most likely answer is still a guess, delivered just as fluently.
    • Training often rewards guessing. A confident answer tends to score better than “I don't know.”

    What keeps it in check

    • Context engineering. Put the right documents, data and instructions in front of the model, so the answer is in its context, not just its memory.
    • Deterministic guardrails. Plain code that checks the output: required citations, data formats, allowed values, rules that block unsupported claims.
    • Tools. Let the model call a calculator, database or search rather than recall.
    • Human review where mistakes are costly. Guardrails can also over-block, as the demo shows with the well-known fact.

    Chapter 14

    Thinking before answering

    Until 2024, the main way to make a model smarter was to train it longer on more text. Reasoning models added a second lever: let it work through the problem in writing before it commits to an answer.

    A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball?

    The fast answer is the one most people blurt out, and the most common pattern in text. Writing out the steps gives the model room to catch it.

    How models learned to reason

    • Chain of thought. Researchers noticed in 2022 that asking a model to “think step by step” improved its answers to math and logic problems.
    • Rewarding right answers. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) was trained with reinforcement learning to produce long internal reasoning before replying. Math and code work well here because answers can be checked automatically.
    • Reasoning can emerge. DeepSeek's R1-Zero (January 2025) learned to double-check and backtrack from reinforcement learning alone, with no examples of reasoning to copy.
    • Thinking costs money. Longer reasoning means more words generated, so slower and more expensive answers. This is called test-time compute.

    From answering to doing

    An agent is a model in a loop: decide on a step, use a tool (search, run code, click a button), read the result, decide the next step. That turns an assistant from something that writes into something that acts.

    Reasoning has its own failure: overthinking. A model can reach the right answer early, keep going, and talk itself out of it.

    Why long tasks are hard: small errors pile up

    Chapter 15

    Looking things up

    Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives a model your documents at the moment you ask. It reuses the word map from Chapter 4: questions and passages become points, and the closest passages get pasted into the prompt.

    What gets added to the prompt

    A made-up employee handbook. Closeness is cosine similarity: it compares the direction two lists of numbers point, ignoring their length, where 1.00 means the same direction. Real systems use hundreds of numbers per passage and fast search indexes to check millions of passages.

    Write documents a retriever can find

    • One topic per document, with a title that says what it covers.
    • A one-sentence summary under each heading. It gives the passage a clear position on the map.
    • Spell out abbreviations the first time. The model does not know your organization's shorthand, as the TDY toggle shows.
    • Describe pictures in words. A retriever cannot search an image.
    • Keep steps numbered and connected (“After step 2, …”), since a passage may be read without its neighbors.

    Check how your tool handles tables

    Many retrieval guides advise turning wide tables into short bulleted statements. Splitters read left to right and can cut a row away from its column headers. Test a few real questions against your own documents before restructuring everything.

    Retrieval fixes missing facts, not reasoning. If the right passage is never found, the model answers from memory anyway, and just as confidently.

    Chapter 16

    When it fails, and who checks

    Language models fail in patterns that don't go away with the next upgrade. Knowing the patterns, and deciding who reviews the output, matters more than any single model choice.

    Case study: forecasting the 2026 World Cup

    In July 2026, researchers had 10 AI assistants predict the entire 2026 FIFA World Cup in advance: 72 group matches, the group tables and the knockout bracket. Getting individual matches right turned out to say almost nothing about getting the tournament right.

    • The bracket decided everything. Total score tracked knockout points almost perfectly (correlation 0.99) and group-match points not at all (0.06).
    • Best at matches, sixth overall. Claude Sonnet 4.6 called the most group results (64%) but finished sixth. GPT-5.5 Thinking ranked eighth on matches but won, as the only model to pick Spain as champion.
    • Confidence meant nothing. Each model rated its own confidence. Those ratings had no relationship with accuracy (correlation −0.06). The most confident model finished seventh.

    Source: Shianifar and Faiud, “AI World Cup 2026,” July 31, 2026. One tournament is a small sample; the pattern, not the ranking, is the lesson.

    Three comforting ideas that don't hold

    • “The next model will fix it.” An upgrade is a different set of odds, not a bug fix. Overall scores improve, but specific tasks can get worse, so every upgrade needs retesting.
    • “People make mistakes too.” A person's mistakes cluster where their knowledge runs out, so their track record predicts their next job. A model's mistakes are jagged: strong on a hard task, wrong on an easy version of it.
    • “The tests pass.” When the same model writes the code and its tests from a vague spec, both can share the same misunderstanding. In 2026, 16 Claude agents built a C compiler that compiled the Linux kernel, yet a basic Hello World failed out of the box because it didn't know where the system's C library was.

    Who should approve AI output

    • Someone who could have made it without AI. Otherwise the review is a guess.
    • An expert from each field it touches, for example legal, technical and business.
    • Label unchecked output as a draft until a person has reviewed it.
    • Keep documentation readable by people. If you need a model to explain a document, you are now checking a second, unverified output.

    Review is the bottleneck
